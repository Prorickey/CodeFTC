# Dead Wheel Odometry

In the Encoder Basics lesson you learned to read a motor's built-in encoder. That works for arm positioning, but using drive-motor encoders to track where your robot is on the field has serious limitations. **Dead wheel odometry** solves most of those problems by using dedicated tracking wheels that do nothing but measure movement.

## What Is a Dead Wheel?

A dead wheel (also called a **tracking wheel** or **odometry wheel**) is a small unpowered wheel that rolls freely against the floor. It is connected directly to an encoder but never to a motor. Because it has no motor fighting against it, it rolls with almost no slip and measures the floor's movement faithfully.

Dead wheels typically use small omni-wheels (typically 35–48 mm diameter) and plug their encoder cables into unused encoder ports on the Control Hub or Expansion Hub.

You read a dead wheel exactly like a motor encoder:

```java
// Dead wheels are wired to motor encoder ports and read as DcMotor objects
DcMotor forwardEncoder = hardwareMap.get(DcMotor.class, "forwardEncoder");
DcMotor strafeEncoder  = hardwareMap.get(DcMotor.class, "strafeEncoder");

int forwardTicks = forwardEncoder.getCurrentPosition();
int strafeTicks  = strafeEncoder.getCurrentPosition();
```

The power line of these "motors" goes unused — only the encoder signal matters.

## Converting Ticks to Distance

The same formula from Encoder Basics applies:

```java
static final double TICKS_PER_REV  = 2000.0; // e.g. REV Through Bore Encoder
static final double WHEEL_DIAMETER = 0.048;   // 48 mm in meters
static final double WHEEL_CIRCUM   = Math.PI * WHEEL_DIAMETER;

double forwardDist = (forwardTicks / TICKS_PER_REV) * WHEEL_CIRCUM;
double strafeDist  = (strafeTicks  / TICKS_PER_REV) * WHEEL_CIRCUM;
```

## Simple 2D Position Tracking

With two perpendicular dead wheels you can integrate position over time:

```java
double x = 0, y = 0;
int prevForward = 0, prevStrafe = 0;

while (opModeIsActive()) {
    int curForward = forwardEncoder.getCurrentPosition();
    int curStrafe  = strafeEncoder.getCurrentPosition();

    double dForward = (curForward - prevForward) / TICKS_PER_REV * WHEEL_CIRCUM;
    double dStrafe  = (curStrafe  - prevStrafe)  / TICKS_PER_REV * WHEEL_CIRCUM;

    y += dForward;  // assuming forward encoder measures Y axis
    x += dStrafe;   // assuming strafe encoder measures X axis

    prevForward = curForward;
    prevStrafe  = curStrafe;

    telemetry.addData("X (m)", "%.3f", x);
    telemetry.addData("Y (m)", "%.3f", y);
    telemetry.update();
}
```

This ignores heading changes — for full field-relative tracking you also need an IMU or a third encoder to measure rotation. But even this simplified version is far more accurate than motor encoders alone on a mecanum robot.

## Localization Option Comparison

There are five common ways to track your robot's position in FTC. Here is how they compare:

### 1. Drive Motor Encoders Only

Use the built-in encoders on your drive motors directly.

**How it works:** Average the left and right encoder readings for forward distance; subtract them for heading. Works well for tank/differential drive. On mecanum drives you need all four wheels.

**Pros:**
- Zero extra hardware or cost
- Already covered in the previous lesson

**Cons:**
- Wheel slip under acceleration, turning, and defense causes significant drift
- Mecanum wheels slip sideways, making Y-axis tracking unreliable
- Motor gearboxes have backlash that adds error over time
- Poor for anything needing sub-field-width accuracy on mecanum

**Best for:** Simple tank-drive autonomous, or when budget is the top constraint.

---

### 2. Two Dead Wheels (2-Wheel Odometry)

Two tracking wheels: one pointing forward (measures Y) and one pointing sideways (measures X strafe).

**How it works:** The forward wheel tracks straight-line distance; the strafe wheel tracks lateral movement. Heading still comes from the IMU.

**Pros:**
- Eliminates drive-wheel slip entirely
- Captures mecanum sideways motion that motor encoders miss
- Low cost (~$15–30 for encoders + wheels)
- Simple to implement

**Cons:**
- Heading accuracy still depends on the IMU, which drifts over a 2-minute match
- Requires careful pod mounting to keep constant floor contact
- No redundancy — if one pod lifts, tracking fails

**Best for:** Budget-conscious teams wanting reliable mecanum odometry without full three-wheel complexity.

---

### 3. Three Dead Wheels (3-Wheel Odometry)

Three tracking wheels: two parallel pods (left and right) for forward motion and heading, plus one perpendicular pod for strafe.

**How it works:** The difference between the two parallel wheels gives you heading without the IMU. All three together give you X, Y, and θ (heading) purely from wheel math.

**Pros:**
- Heading is derived entirely from encoders, not the IMU — eliminates gyro drift
- Full 2D pose (x, y, heading) from encoders alone
- Very accurate over a full 2-minute match
- Used by the majority of high-performing FTC teams

**Cons:**
- More complex mounting — three pods must all maintain floor contact
- Slightly more code to fuse the three readings into a pose
- Adds ~$30–50 cost for a third encoder and pod

**Best for:** Teams wanting the most accurate pure-encoder localization available. The industry standard for high-level FTC.

---

### 4. goBILDA Pinpoint Odometry Computer

The [goBILDA Pinpoint](https://www.gobilda.com/pinpoint-odometry-computer/) is a dedicated I2C board that accepts two encoder cables and handles all the odometry math internally.

**How it works:** You mount the Pinpoint on your robot with one encoder forward and one strafe (exactly like 2-wheel odometry), then communicate via I2C. The Pinpoint returns a ready-to-use X/Y/heading pose at up to 250 Hz. It has a built-in IMU that it fuses with the two encoders automatically.

```java
// Pinpoint is accessed via its own SDK driver
GoBildaPinpointDriver odometry = hardwareMap.get(GoBildaPinpointDriver.class, "pinpoint");
odometry.resetPosAndIMU();

// In your loop:
odometry.update();
double x       = odometry.getPosX();   // meters
double y       = odometry.getPosY();   // meters
double heading = odometry.getHeading(); // radians
```

**Pros:**
- Plug-and-play: no manual odometry math required
- Onboard IMU fusion gives better heading than 2-wheel alone
- Clean API — one call gives a full pose
- Physically compact (~35 g)
- Costs ~$35

**Cons:**
- Only two encoder inputs (still IMU-dependent for heading, unlike 3-wheel)
- Requires goBILDA-specific SDK driver classes
- Adds an I2C device to your hub (limited I2C ports)

**Best for:** Teams who want reliable odometry without writing their own math, and are comfortable with a proprietary driver.

---

### 5. SparkFun OTOS (Optical Tracking Odometry Sensor)

The [SparkFun OTOS](https://www.sparkfun.com/products/24904) uses an **optical flow sensor** — the same technology as a computer mouse — to track movement directly from the floor surface, with no wheels at all.

**How it works:** A small camera and laser look straight down at the floor ~10 mm away. The optical sensor detects pixel movement between frames and integrates it into an X/Y/heading pose. An onboard IMU assists with heading. Communicates via I2C.

```java
SparkFunOTOS otos = hardwareMap.get(SparkFunOTOS.class, "sensor_otos");
otos.calibrateImu();
otos.resetTracking();

// In your loop:
SparkFunOTOS.Pose2D pos = otos.getPosition();
double x       = pos.x;   // inches by default
double y       = pos.y;
double heading = pos.h;   // degrees
```

**Pros:**
- No wheels to slip, lift off the floor, or maintain contact
- Nothing mechanical — extremely durable
- Clean pose API identical to Pinpoint
- Works on any flooring (field tiles, carpet, smooth concrete)
- Costs ~$55

**Cons:**
- Optical flow can fail on very shiny or featureless surfaces
- Less proven at the highest FTC competition levels than 3-wheel odometry
- Floor height must be consistent (~8–12 mm from sensor to floor)
- Heading still has some IMU drift, though better than most

**Best for:** Teams who want zero-slip localization without mechanical encoder pods, or whose robot design makes mounting dead wheel pods difficult.

## Comparison Summary

| Method | Cost | Accuracy | Heading Source | Complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drive motor encoders | Free | Low–Medium | IMU | Low |
| 2-wheel dead wheels | ~$20–30 | Medium–High | IMU | Low–Medium |
| 3-wheel dead wheels | ~$40–60 | High | Encoders (no IMU) | Medium |
| goBILDA Pinpoint | ~$35 | Medium–High | IMU + encoder fusion | Low |
| SparkFun OTOS | ~$55 | Medium–High | IMU + optical | Low |

**Recommendation for most teams:** Start with 3-wheel dead wheels if you have the budget and want the best accuracy for autonomous. Use Pinpoint or OTOS if you want a simpler plug-and-play solution. Use motor encoders only for very simple autos or tank-drive robots.

In this exercise you will read two dead wheel encoder values and compute the estimated forward and strafe distances traveled.

Two dead wheels are registered in the hardware map as `"forwardEncoder"` and `"strafeEncoder"`. The forward encoder reads **2000 ticks** and the strafe encoder reads **500 ticks**.

Use these constants:
```java
static final double TICKS_PER_REV = 2000.0;
static final double WHEEL_DIAMETER = 0.048; // meters
```

Your task:
1. Get both encoders from `hardwareMap`.
2. After `waitForStart()`, read both tick counts.
3. Compute `forwardDist` and `strafeDist` in meters using the constants above.
4. Display both on telemetry with captions `"Forward (m)"` and `"Strafe (m)"`, using `"%.4f"` format.
5. Call `telemetry.update()`.

The forward distance should be approximately **0.1508 m** (one full revolution × 48 mm circumference) and the strafe distance should be approximately **0.0377 m**.
